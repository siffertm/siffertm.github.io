Learning is one of my favourite things. I've started over so many times that I can't remember the exact count anymore. Different sports, different fields, different countries, different languages. Each time, I told myself this was the one where I'd finally "figure it out."

Each time, I was terrified I wouldn't be good enough.

## Why I Keep Restarting

The reason I keep starting over is simple: I'm fascinated by how things work. Not in an abstract way, but in a practical way that lets me understand my environment better and connect with people around me.

How do people actually function? That led me to psychology and philosophy. How does the internet work? That sent me down the path of coding, computer architecture, and networking. Why are athletes so special? That became neuroscience, biomechanics, and understanding both body and mind.

The goal was never learning for learning's sake. It was always answering my own obsessive questions. Those personal questions became my roadmap.

But here's what I didn't expect: by restarting so many times, I became an expert at something else entirely - being a beginner.

## The Imposter Syndrome That Almost Stopped Me

When I decided to learn coding, I hit a wall immediately. Coming from a Literature and teaching background, the jump to logical thinking and mathematics felt impossible. I remember sitting in front of my screen thinking: "I'm not good enough for this. I'm too bad at maths. I don't think like a programmer."

The imposter syndrome wasn't a whisper. It was a scream.

When things got difficult - and they always do in the early stages - I wanted to quit. The code didn't make sense. The logic felt foreign. Every tutorial seemed to assume knowledge I didn't have. I genuinely believed I was the problem, not the learning curve.

What saved me wasn't willpower or some innate talent for coding. It was community.

I joined Discord servers full of people learning to code. I started at 42 Berlin where I was surrounded by others struggling with the same concepts. I talked to people who had felt exactly what I was feeling. They told me their breakthrough moments. They showed me that the steep learning curve was normal, not a sign I didn't belong.

The community didn't make the difficulty disappear. But it made the difficulty *survivable*.

## The Comparison Trap That Almost Killed My Progress

For years, I learned by comparing myself to others. And of course, I always compared myself to the best. The programmer who solved problems elegantly. The athlete with perfect form. The student who seemed to understand everything immediately.

I was always failing at that comparison. Always.

The turning point came when I stopped looking at where the masters were and started paying attention to where *I* was yesterday compared to where I am today. The focus shifted from "Am I as good as them?" to "Am I better than I was last week?"

That's when everything changed.

## The Learning Curve: Week One vs Week Twelve

When you start something new, the learning curve is steep. In week one, everything feels impossible. You're overwhelmed by how much you don't know. Every concept feels alien. Progress feels non-existent.

By week four, things start clicking. You have a mental model forming. You can do simple things without thinking. You feel like you're getting somewhere.

But here's what I've learned from starting over more than ten times: the more things you learn, the more you realize how little you actually know about any given topic.

I've studied coding, neuroscience, psychology, multiple sports, languages, cybersecurity. The pattern is always the same. You learn enough to think you understand. Then you go deeper and realize you barely scratched the surface.

So I stopped measuring learning by how much I know. I started measuring it by whether I'm improving a little bit every day. One small thing today. One small thing tomorrow. That consistency matters infinitely more than dramatic breakthroughs.

## The Secret Weapon: Doing It

I gathered books, took courses, watched tutorials, listened to podcasts. But nothing actually stuck until I started *using* what I learned.

For coding, this meant building small projects. For psychology, it meant having conversations with people and observing patterns. For sports, it meant training myself and others. For cybersecurity, it meant analyzing real incidents and explaining concepts to people who didn't have technical backgrounds.

The learning you do in your head is different from the learning you do with your hands.

When I teach people what I've learned - through presentations, workshops, or one-on-one coaching - I understand it at a completely different level than when I just read about it. Explaining forces you to organize your knowledge. It reveals the gaps. It makes abstract concepts concrete.

## Starting Over Isn't Starting From Zero

Sure, I've started in completely different fields multiple times. Coding was a stretch from Literature. Neuroscience was a stretch from pure psychology. Brazilian Ju-Jitsu was a stretch from judo and football.

But here's the truth: I've never really started from zero.

When I learned coding, I already spoke English, which meant I could read documentation and watch tutorials in their original language. That single advantage accelerated my learning by months.

When I studied neuroscience, I already knew psychology. I understood what motivation was, how the brain responds to stress, how learning happens. Neuroscience became a deeper exploration of things I already partially understood.

Over years of learning, I built a database of knowledge across different domains. Now when I encounter something new, I don't start from zero. I start by finding the connections to what I already know.

Cybersecurity connects to coding and how computers work. German connects to French teaching and learning other languages. Brazilian Ju-Jitsu connects to all the physical training I'd done before. Presentations connect to all the teaching and feedback I'd received.

Every new thing you learn makes the next new thing easier.

## Why This Matters for Your Life Right Now

I'm telling you this story because I work with people at universities, companies, and startups who are facing the same thing I faced. They're considering a career change. They're thinking about learning a new skill. They're wondering if they're too old, too inexperienced, too far behind, too bad at the technical parts.

They're comparing themselves to people ten years into their journey and wondering why they can't do what those people do after three months.

What I've learned from starting over more than ten times is this: the people who succeed at starting over aren't naturally talented. They're not math geniuses or born learners or people for whom everything comes easily.

They're people who found community. People who stopped comparing themselves to masters. People who started doing, not just learning. People who improved a little bit every day and trusted that consistency would matter more than perfection.

## The Role of Community in Starting Over

I want to emphasize this because it was the difference between me quitting coding and me sticking with it.

When you're learning something difficult, you will hit moments of doubt. You'll think you're not cut out for this. You'll see others progressing faster. You'll want to give up.

In those moments, community is the difference between pushing through and walking away.

The people in my Discord server who had already struggled through the same problems. The students at 42 Berlin who were facing the same confusion I was facing.

They showed me that the difficulty was normal. That the doubt was part of the process. That the steep learning curve wasn't proof I didn't belong - it was proof I was actually learning.

If you're considering starting something new, find your community first. Join online forums, take classes where you'll meet other learners, connect with people who've done what you want to do. This single step is more important than finding the perfect course or the best resources.

## Starting Over as a Superpower

By now, I've noticed something. The more times you start over, the better you get at it. The curve gets faster. The fear becomes more manageable. You develop intuition for what works and what doesn't.

You realize that being a beginner isn't a weakness. It's a temporary state. And each time you cycle through it, you come out with new capabilities.

I used to see starting over as failure - proof that I couldn't commit to one thing. Now I see it as a strength. I've learned how to learn. I've learned how to find community. I've learned how to stay consistent when everything feels impossible.

More importantly, I've learned that these skills transfer. The person I am when starting something new in cybersecurity is fundamentally different from the person I was when starting coding. I'm not more talented or more intelligent. But I'm less afraid. More patient. Better at asking for help.

And I can help other people navigate that same journey because I've walked it multiple times.

## The Question You Should Be Asking

If you're at a crossroads in your career or your life, if you're considering learning something new or making a change, the real question isn't "Am I good enough?"

The question is: "Do I have community around me? Am I practicing what I learn, not just consuming information? Am I comparing myself to where I was yesterday, or to where masters are today? Am I improving something small every single day?"

Those are the things that actually determine whether you'll succeed at starting over.

The talent, the background, the natural ability - those matter less than you think.

But the community, the practice, the perspective, the consistency - those matter more than everything else combined.
